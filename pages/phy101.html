PHY 101

Introduction to Physics

12 Lectures Beginner Level Physics

Course Overview

This course provides a comprehensive introduction to classical physics, focusing on the fundamental principles that govern motion, forces, energy, and momentum. Students will develop a strong conceptual understanding of mechanics while learning to apply mathematical techniques to solve physics problems.

The course emphasizes both theoretical understanding and practical problem-solving skills. Through systematic exploration of kinematics, dynamics, work, energy, and momentum, students will build the foundation necessary for advanced physics courses and applications in engineering, technology, and the physical sciences.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand fundamental concepts of motion, including displacement, velocity, and acceleration
  • Apply Newton's laws of motion to analyze forces and predict object behavior
  • Solve problems involving work, energy, and power in mechanical systems
  • Understand conservation of momentum and apply it to collision problems
  • Analyze rotational motion and relate it to translational motion concepts
  • Develop problem-solving strategies for physics applications
  • Build mathematical fluency in physics contexts using calculus and algebra

Prerequisites

Mathematics: Solid understanding of algebra, trigonometry, and basic calculus (derivatives and integrals). Familiarity with vectors is helpful but will be reviewed.

Prior Knowledge: No previous physics coursework required. This course is designed for students encountering university-level physics for the first time.

Lecture Series

Lecture 1

Introduction and Units of Measurement

Overview of physics as a discipline, the scientific method, and the importance of standardized units. Introduction to SI units, dimensional analysis, and significant figures.

45 minutes
Lecture 2

Vectors and Coordinate Systems

Introduction to vectors in physics. Vector addition, subtraction, and scalar multiplication. Cartesian coordinate systems and vector components.

50 minutes
Lecture 3

One-Dimensional Kinematics

Position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration in one dimension. Motion with constant acceleration. Free fall as a special case of constant acceleration.

55 minutes
Lecture 4

Two-Dimensional Motion

Extension of kinematics to two dimensions. Projectile motion and circular motion. Velocity and acceleration vectors in 2D motion problems.

60 minutes
Lecture 5

Newton's Laws of Motion

Introduction to force as a vector quantity. Newton's three laws of motion and their applications. Inertial reference frames and the concept of mass.

55 minutes
Lecture 6

Applications of Newton's Laws

Problem-solving with Newton's laws. Free-body diagrams, friction forces, tension, and normal forces. Connected objects and inclined planes.

60 minutes
Lecture 7

Work and Kinetic Energy

Definition of work done by a force. Kinetic energy and the work-energy theorem. Power as the rate of doing work. Applications to mechanical systems.

50 minutes
Lecture 8

Potential Energy and Conservation of Energy

Gravitational and elastic potential energy. Conservative and non-conservative forces. Conservation of mechanical energy in isolated systems.

55 minutes
Lecture 9

Linear Momentum and Impulse

Definition of momentum and impulse. Impulse-momentum theorem. Conservation of momentum in isolated systems. Center of mass motion.

50 minutes
Lecture 10

Collisions

Elastic and inelastic collisions in one and two dimensions. Conservation laws applied to collision problems. Coefficient of restitution.

55 minutes
Lecture 11

Rotational Kinematics

Angular position, velocity, and acceleration. Rotational motion with constant angular acceleration. Relationship between linear and angular quantities.

50 minutes
Lecture 12

Rotational Dynamics and Angular Momentum

Torque and rotational inertia. Newton's second law for rotation. Angular momentum and its conservation. Rolling motion as combined translation and rotation.

60 minutes

Course Information

This introductory physics course requires approximately 6-8 hours of study per week including lecture viewing and problem-solving practice.
